# Copy — only a costly signal is credible

A reader can't check most claims, so they weigh each one by what it would cost the writer to make it falsely. A signal separates the true from the false only when faking it costs more than it earns (Spence 1973); a claim that costs nothing to make is cheap talk, and readers discount it to what any competitor could equally say (Crawford & Sobel 1982). "Fast", "secure", "trusted by thousands" are free to write, so they carry nothing.

- **Attach the proof that costs something to fake.** A named customer who could deny it, a number with its method and source, a guarantee that costs you when it fails, a price shown before the signup, a free tier that lets the product argue for itself, a public status page.
- **A claim with no costly proof available gets cut or made specific.** "Secure" becomes the named control; "fast" becomes the measured figure. A specific claim is falsifiable, which is what makes it cost something.
- **Copy selects its reader.** Wording that states the price, the constraint or the audience up front turns away the visitor who wouldn't convert and keeps the one who would — scammers write implausible stories on purpose, so only the most credulous reply (Herley 2012). Write the filter in when a wrong-fit sign-up costs more than a missed one.
- **The page's promise sets the user's expectation of the product.** A claim the product doesn't keep converts once and churns; the user who arrived on it meets the product as a broken promise.
