A mark's job is to **identify** — it cannot say everything, and one that tries says nothing (Identify, Conservation International). Every rule below serves that job. Cites are to printed pages; *Identify* is cited by case.

## 1. The brief answers before a line is drawn
You have no client in the room, so these come from the brief. One the brief leaves open is an assumption you name in your return — never a question you silently answer with a drawing.
- **The design problem**, in a sentence: new name, too complex, needs to unify parts, reads weakly where it lives (Identify, Introduction).
- **What it is and who meets it** — what it does, for whom, how it wants to be seen (Airey pp. 44–46). The adjectives the owner wants on it steer the form (Airey p. 56).
- **The name people actually say.** Design for the colloquial name and cut what nobody says — "The", legal suffixes (Identify, Pan Am; Library of Congress; Airey p. 51). Read the name for a visual idea in it — a pun, a hidden letter, an abbreviation — and for how it sounds and misreads (Identify, Grey; Showtime; Do@).
- **Where it lives most.** Design for that spot: a tab strip, a README, an avatar beside forty others (Identify, HarperCollins; Old Chatham).
- **What already exists** — the current mark and its equity. Tweak before replacing; discard equity only for a stated reason (Identify, Armani Exchange; National Geographic; Wheeler p. 92).
- **The neighbours** — marks it sits beside in its category; aim at what they do weakly (Airey p. 46).

## 2. Choose the form before drawing it
The first question is whether it needs a symbol at all, or only a memorable treatment of the name (Identify, Introduction). The branch is in [`reference/form-and-type.md`](reference/form-and-type.md) — load it now, and again for the wordmark and lockups of the kit.

## 3. Concepts: rough, then few, then real
- **Rough the idea out loosely first**, then refine; settling a concept with precision tools locks in the first form that came out (Identify, Introduction).
- **Each concept is a different idea with its own rationale** — up to three routes, plus the one you prefer and why (Airey p. 47). When one idea is plainly right, show it and its variations instead of padding the set (Identify, HarperCollins).
- **Show only concepts that can work** — a weak one included for contrast is one the user may pick (Identify, Introduction).
- **Single colour first.** Shape carries recognition, colour second, words third (Wheeler p. 50); a mark works in black-and-white and in colour (Wheeler p. 28). Colour can *be* the idea, and the mark still identifies without it (Identify, Grey).
- **Shown in use, not on blank paper.** A mark judged alone on white gets rejected and the same mark approved in context (Identify, Armani Exchange). Put each concept where it lives: the tab, the README header, the avatar, the app tile.

## 4. Judge it — the test
Judge on what works, never on taste: **appropriate, simple, memorable** (Identify, Introduction).
- **Appropriate** — relevant in form and concept; not literal. The mark is a vessel that takes on meaning with use (Identify, Introduction; Chase Manhattan).
- **Simple** — one story, an uncomplicated form, so it survives every size and medium (Identify, Introduction).
- **Memorable** — distinctive. Simplicity and distinction pull against each other: the simpler the form, the less special it gets, so push along that line rather than stopping at plain (Identify, Introduction).

Two checks that make the test concrete:
- **Draw it from memory** after a glance — if you can't, it isn't simple enough (Identify, Introduction).
- **Could anyone else use it?** A mark easy to imagine on another organisation is not specific enough (Identify, Library of Congress).

Then Wheeler's list as a sweep: bold, memorable, appropriate, protectable, recognisable, enduring, works across media and scale, works in black-and-white and colour (Wheeler p. 28).

**Small sizes come from the master's simplicity.** Draw the master simple enough to hold at the smallest size; where it can't, the icon is a **shorthand** pulled from it — one letter or one part — not a shrunken copy (Identify, American Republic; El Mall; Do@).

## 5. Present for a pick
- Walk through each concept's idea so it can be judged on its thinking, not its surface (Wheeler p. 32; Identify, Bank of Taipei). Every route gets a rationale, not only the favourite (Wheeler p. 48).
- **A new mark feels foreign at first.** Say so with the set: a first reaction ("I don't like blue") is not a verdict, and testing a mark on gut response rejects the unfamiliar (Identify, Introduction; Conservation International).
- In a redesign, show the old mark becoming the new one so attachment carries over (Identify, Conservation International).

## Traps
- **The category cliché** — the eagle, the human figure for a nonprofit, the athlete for sport (Identify, Hearst; Conservation International; WTA). Two stock motifs fused into one new idea is the way out of one (Identify, American Republic).
- **Depicting the building, or anything that can disappear** — it says nothing about what the org does and dates with the thing (Identify, Library of Congress; Alliance for Downtown New York).
- **Detail creep.** Count the parts and cut; negative space does the work extra strokes were doing (Identify, NBC). Reduce the concept too — a basic element carries more meaning than a specific one (Identify, HarperCollins).
- **Decoration without a reason.** A symbol added because marks have symbols fights the name (Identify, Merck; Shinsegae).
- **An ownable-looking common shape.** A circle, a check, a letter in a square can't be owned; note that a search is due before anyone commits (Identify, Amesco; NBC).
- **Trends and characters** date first (Wheeler p. 64).
