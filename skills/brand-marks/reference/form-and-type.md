# Form, wordmarks and lockups

## Choosing the form
The categories — wordmark, letterform, emblem, pictorial, abstract — blur (Wheeler pp. 48–49). The book treats them as an even choice; the product owner's house rule outranks it. **The default form is a symbol anchored in the product's own subject**: what it is or what it does (weather for a weather app, money moving for a payments app), so that someone who has never heard of the product recognises the subject at a glance. It is then drawn with the geometry, solid-mass, gap and thumbnail rules in `SKILL.md` §3. This is Wheeler's pictorial mark, which reads fast in a cluttered setting (Identify, Old Chatham). The branch is the name and where the mark lives:

- **No one-letter or monogram mark**, and no letter as the app icon or the small-size shorthand, unless the brief asks for one. A lone letter is a lazy default: nothing in it comes from the product.
- **A short, distinctive name → a wordmark** alone is often enough (Identify, Introduction; Dime). The house rule allows it when the brief asks for a wordmark-only kit or the name alone is the mark.
- **A long or unwieldy name, or parts to unify → a symbol** that stands in for it; a long wordmark would have to shrink into small spaces (Identify, HarperCollins; Merck; Chase Manhattan).
- **A generic name → treat one part of it visually** to make it distinctive (Identify, Showtime; Centro).
- **Initials → only when people already use them and the brief asks for an initials mark**, and never one letter pulled out of an acronym people say whole (Identify, EPA; NBC).
- **An abstract mark needs a reason stated in the brief or named as an assumption in your return**, such as a broad or technical offer with no object that stands for it. It is the hardest form to do well and gains meaning through exposure (Wheeler p. 58; Identify, Chase Manhattan).
- **A name inside a shape** is the hardest form to read small (Wheeler p. 60) — unless the name is set tight to the edges of a square tile, which holds on stickers and poster corners (Identify, Mount Sinai; UNDP; Downtown Alliance). A ring of type around a symbol keeps a bare version with the type moved below for small uses (Identify, Bicentennial). A plain disc behind the mark is different: it shields it on dark or busy ground (Identify, PBS; WTA; Smithsonian; Mobil).
- **Beside other marks it would fight** — sub-brands, partner logos — drop the symbol (Identify, Hearst).

### Explore the subject before choosing
The product owner's house rule, not the books'.

- **A real object qualifies only when it is the subject or plainly stands for it without explanation.** A cultural reference, an insider reference, a pun or a metaphor the viewer has to be told fails (*Traps* in `SKILL.md`).
- **Draw at least four genuinely different concepts, each built from the subject.** Each is in solid mass, rendered at 128 and 48 px, with one line on what it shows of the subject.
- **Give each a stranger check** beside appropriate / simple / memorable: would someone who doesn't know the product read the subject?
- **Judge each in the home-screen tile too** (`SKILL.md` §3). Prefer shapes that fill a square. A wide, thin or tall, thin silhouette is reframed: a closer view, a top-down or three-quarter view, or the one part that carries the idea. If it can't be reframed, it loses to an object that fills the square.
- **The common category glyph doesn't count as one of the four** (*Traps* in `SKILL.md`). The parts may be the subject's own; the idea that combines them must be the product's.
- **Then narrow to the routes and name your preferred one.** Keep up to three that can work as routes, mark the one you prefer and say why, and give each its appropriate / simple / memorable / stranger verdict. Log every concept you drew in your return, with a one-line reason for each one you dropped (`SKILL.md` §5).

## Wordmarks
- **Simple lettering plus one device** — the red O, the dot, the check (Identify, Mobil; Dime). Shown twice where once goes unnoticed (Identify, Gemini). The strongest devices **remove** rather than add: a missing crossbar or spine, the gap carrying the idea (Identify, Centro; WTA; Factset).
- **Hand-tune it.** Spacing, stem weight and stroke width are where a set word becomes a mark; the tweaks go unnoticed and make the effect (Identify, Barneys; Showtime).
- **Letters share the symbol's stroke and geometry** (Identify, Rockefeller Center; Time Warner). A face with a meaningful origin beats a neutral one when one exists (Identify, Library of Congress; PBS).
- **Stacking or a divider forces the right reading** of a name that misreads run together (Identify, MIAD; SEW Infrastructure).
- **The letter traits grow into an alphabet** for product and sub-brand names (Identify, Opet; Mobil; Harvard Maintenance).

## Lockups
- **The signature is a fixed relationship** of mark, name and any tagline, in horizontal and vertical versions (Wheeler p. 48).
- **Symbol height = the name block**, from the first line's cap top to the last line's baseline: a two-line name gets a two-line-tall symbol, and further lines hang below without the symbol growing (Identify, Library of Congress; Conservation International; National Geographic; Smithsonian; MBTA).
- **A secondary name is set apart by one contrast** — smaller and lighter, aligned to the name's edge rather than the symbol's (Identify, Merck; Hearst), or the same size in another colour or italic (Identify, Grey; Harvard Maintenance; Smithsonian; National Geographic).
- **Symbol and wordmark also stand apart.** A lockup too crowded for a space is split, not shrunk (Identify, Mobil; Beehouse).
- **Ship each colour positive and reversed in a box**, as a set (Identify, Showtime; El Mall).
- **The app icon** takes the symbol: one part of it, or the whole symbol simplified, never a letter unless the brief asks (house rule). The symbol spans most of the safe area in width and height (`SKILL.md` §3). A wordmark fits the tile only if it stays legible at tile size (Wheeler p. 75).
