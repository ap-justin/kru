# Security — the attacker runs a business

An attacker spends money, time and risk to earn a return, and picks targets where the yield beats the cost — scammers filter for the most profitable victims rather than the most numerous (Herley 2012). Most real abuse is no exploit at all: it is volume, pushed through a path that has to stay open to honest users.

- **Three numbers per abuse case: gain, cost per attempt, precondition** — gain and precondition as `code-reviewer`'s incentive bullet defines them. Weight by the attacker's cost, never by the attack's sophistication: a cheap, anonymous, no-precondition attempt repeated a million times outranks a clever one.
- **Make abuse unprofitable where it can't be made impossible.** Sign-in, sign-up, password reset, card entry, a coupon field, an invite, anything that sends an SMS or email — each must stay open, so the lever is cost per attempt. Rate-limit on what the attacker has to pay for (an account, a verified phone, a card, a paid IP) rather than on what's free; add friction — a challenge, a delay, a verification — where the attempt volume is, after the first failure rather than on every visit.
- **Find the paths that pay the attacker directly.** A path whose side effect earns them money gets farmed: an OTP or invite SMS to numbers whose carrier shares the fee with them (SMS pumping — the codes are never entered, because no user exists), a sign-up bonus, a referral credit, a free trial with a paid resource behind it, a card-authorization endpoint (card testing — the card-rail seats' `## Money rules` carry it). These need a cap per actor the attacker can't mint for free.
- **Price the defense on the honest user.** A challenge on every sign-in taxes everyone every day; on the fourth failure it taxes only the attacker. A defense whose aggregate cost to users exceeds the harm it prevents gets rejected or routed around (Herley 2009).
- **Put the loss on the party that holds the control.** Security fails where the one who could prevent a loss isn't the one who bears it (Anderson 2001; Anderson & Moore 2006). When a design leaves a user's or merchant's loss behind a control only you hold, the incentive to maintain that control is missing — name it, so it gets owned.
